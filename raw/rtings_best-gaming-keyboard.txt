To find the best gaming keyboard for your needs, consider the games you play, the keys you need, and your budget. Low latency matters most for competitive gaming, while magnetic switches with adjustable actuation and Rapid Trigger give you more control over when keypresses register and reset. For casual gaming, these features are less important, and you may want to focus more on comfort or customizable RGB lighting, depending on your tastes.

Before choosing, consider a few practical trade-offs:

Size: Smaller keyboards leave more room for mouse movements, but compact layouts can remove the numpad, function row, or dedicated arrow keys. Consider which keys you regularly use in games and everyday tasks.
Connectivity: Wired and wireless receiver connections are best for gaming. Bluetooth is convenient for switching between devices and typically provides better battery life than a receiver connection, but it has higher latency, making it a poor connection type for competitive play.
Switches: Conventional mechanical switches offer a range of feels and sound profiles. Magnetic switches add adjustable input settings that can be particularly useful in fast-paced titles like competitive FPS and rhythm games, though they aren't necessary for a responsive gaming experience.

We've tested over 300 keyboards, and our recommendations below cover a range of budgets, sizes, and gaming needs. If you're shopping on a tighter budget, see our best budget gaming keyboards. If you already know which size you want, see our picks for the best 60%, 75%, and TKL keyboards.

How We Test Keyboards

We test dozens of keyboards each year, and to maintain independence, we buy every unit ourselves. Our reviews go beyond subjective typing impressions or quick gaming sessions. Each keyboard is put through the same standardized test bench, so you can directly compare our results. We use specialized tools like a Mecmesin force tester to measure switch force curves and a Beagle 480 USB analyzer to capture input data for latency testing. Multiple teams contribute to each review in a process that can take days or even weeks from start to finish.

The Wooting 80HE is the best gaming keyboard we've tested. It combines exceptionally low and consistent latency with magnetic switches that give you precise control over when each keypress registers and resets, which can give you a small but meaningful edge, especially if you play fast-paced or competitive titles. You can adjust the actuation distance of each key individually, setting shallow presses for quick inputs or deeper presses to help avoid accidental inputs. Rapid Trigger lets keys reset with a small upward movement rather than returning to a fixed reset point, perfect for quick direction changes in FPS games or repeated inputs in rhythm games.

Available as a web-based client or a downloadable local app, Wooting's software is intuitive, easy to navigate, and offers extensive customization. Advanced features include SOCD, which controls how opposing directional inputs are handled when pressed together, and Analog output, which can mimic gamepad or joystick inputs for gradual steering or throttle control in compatible racing games and flight sims. Wooting's strong track record for long-term product support is another highlight, with frequent performance tweaks and new features for older models, plus a four-year warranty. We tested the heavier, more premium zinc-alloy case option, but the 80HE is also available with a less expensive ABS plastic case.

The Corsair K70 CORE is our pick for the best budget gaming keyboard. It's a great fit if you want a straightforward full-size mechanical keyboard with outstanding gaming performance. You can buy it with or without a wrist rest for added palm support. Its biggest trade-off compared to the more expensive Wooting 80HE above is that its mechanical switches don't support adjustable actuation or Rapid Trigger. Corsair also sells this keyboard in a smaller TKL (TenKeyLess) version, which removes the numpad to free up desk space, giving FPS players and those with smaller setups more room for mouse movement. It feels sturdy, thanks to a chassis made from a mix of plastic and aluminum that minimizes deck flex. The switches are factory-lubed, and some sound-dampening materials are included to help reduce the rattle and ping of typing.

The K70 CORE uses Corsair MLX Red linear switches, which are lightweight and have a short pre-travel distance, making them well-suited for fast-paced and reaction-focused games. Its 1000Hz polling rate falls below the 8000Hz offered by some newer keyboards, like the Wooting above, but most people are unlikely to notice the difference during gameplay. The switches are also soldered to the board, so replacing them to change the typing feel requires desoldering. The shine-through keycaps and bright RGB lighting make the keyboard easy to use in low-light environments.

The SteelSeries Apex 3 TKL is our pick for the best cheap gaming keyboard. It's a good fit if you want an inexpensive, quiet keyboard for casual gaming. Its latency is higher than that of our more expensive picks, so we don't recommend it for competitive play, but it's well-suited for casual gaming where precise timing is less important. Its TenKeyLess form factor leaves plenty of space on your desk for sweeping mouse movements. Unlike the other keyboards we recommend on this list, which use mechanical or magnetic switches, this keyboard has rubber dome switches. These are comfortable and fairly light to press, but their main advantage is that they're extremely quiet, making this keyboard a great option for shared spaces. However, their somewhat mushy bottom-out feel may be less satisfying if you prefer the feel of mechanical switches.

It also has some useful extras, including the ability to set macros to any key, dedicated media controls, and a volume knob. It has RGB backlighting as well, though the keys aren't individually addressable, which means you can only customize the lighting in eight zones. If you need a full-size layout with a numpad, consider the SteelSeries Apex 3 instead. It's very similar apart from its size, but it includes a wrist rest and is typically only a bit more expensive.

The NuPhy WH80 is the best wireless gaming keyboard we've tested. It delivers exceptional gaming performance comparable to the Wooting 80HE above. Like the Wooting, it uses magnetic switches with adjustable actuation and Rapid Trigger. Rapid Trigger in particular is useful for quick repeated inputs and direction changes in FPS and rhythm games. It also supports 8000Hz polling over its wireless receiver, helping keep latency low and consistent. If you're new to these settings, the software includes presets so you can try them without configuring everything yourself.

It has outstanding build quality, with a sturdy-feeling plastic case and aluminum accents, and it delivers very smooth, stable keystrokes. Multiple layers of acoustic material inside the case keep keystrokes quiet with a pleasing sound profile. It also supports Bluetooth for switching between up to three paired devices, though the receiver connection is best for gaming. NuPhy advertises up to 500 hours of wireless battery life, which is quite good for a gaming keyboard of this size and performance.

The main trade-offs are price, warranty coverage, and software usability. The WH80 costs roughly as much as the Wooting's more premium zinc-alloy case option and more than its ABS plastic version. NuPhy also provides a shorter one-year warranty, compared with Wooting's four years. While NuPhy's software is well laid out and explains its advanced functions clearly, it isn't quite as intuitive to use as Wooting's.

For a less expensive wireless option, we recommend the Keychron K2 Ultra 8K. It feels slightly less premium and doesn't quite match the gaming performance of the NuPhy WH80 above, but it still delivers exceptionally low latency suitable for competitive play. The main trade-off is that, unlike the NuPhy's magnetic switches, its conventional mechanical switches don't support adjustable actuation or Rapid Trigger. If you aren't interested in these features, it delivers straightforwardly excellent gaming performance at a much lower price.

It supports 8000Hz polling over wired and 2.4GHz receiver connections, while Bluetooth lets you pair with up to three devices. Web-based software provides key remapping, macros, and backlighting customization. You can choose linear or tactile switches, and its hot-swappable PCB lets you change the typing feel without soldering. Its thick aluminum frame helps it feel sturdy, though the underside is plastic. Its stock keycaps also lack shine-through legends, making them harder to read in dim lighting. We tested the compact 75% K2 Ultra 8K, but the same series includes TKL, 96%, and full-size models if you need a different layout.

We recommend the MonsGeek FUN60 Ultra as the best budget wireless gaming keyboard we've tested. It offers the best performance for the price, with exceptionally low latency and magnetic switches that support adjustable actuation, Rapid Trigger, and SOCD. However, its main limitation compared to our more expensive wireless picks is that its compact 60% layout lacks a dedicated function row and arrow keys, which may be a dealbreaker for some. That said, it also has a solid aluminum case, which is a very surprising feature at this price point. Software polish is another compromise. Its downloadable software doesn't match the polish or ease of use of Wooting's or NuPhy's platforms. MonsGeek also provides a one-year warranty, matching NuPhy's coverage but falling well short of Wooting's four.

We tested the wireless TMR sensor version, which supports both magnetic and conventional mechanical switches. Other configurations have different connectivity options and sensor types, so check the listing carefully to ensure you're buying a wireless model, and see our full review for more details on variants. Retail availability has also been spotty, so we recommend checking MonsGeek's official product page and possibly signing up for restock notifications if it's currently unavailable.

Notable Mentions
SteelSeries Apex Pro TKL (2023): 

The SteelSeries Apex Pro TKL (2023) uses magnetic switches with adjustable actuation, like the Wooting 80HE. It's also a bit less expensive, and it has a programmable OLED screen. Unfortunately, it doesn't perform as well, and both the build quality and typing quality aren't as good.

 See our review
Wooting 60HE v2: 

The Wooting 60HE v2 is a smaller sibling to the Wooting 80HE and is a great alternative if you like everything the 80HE offers but prefer a smaller 60% layout. It doesn't have a more premium zinc-alloy case option, but due to its standard design, it also offers more hardware customizability and compatibility with a wide array of aftermarket cases.

 See our review
NuPhy Air75 HE: 

The NuPhy Air75 HE is a strong alternative to the Wooting 80HE if you prefer a low-profile keyboard. It offers comparable gaming performance and similar core features, while its slimmer design makes it more comfortable to use without a wrist rest. However, its software isn't as easy to use, and its warranty is much shorter.

 See our review
MonsGeek M1 V5 TMR: 

The MonsGeek M1 V5 TMR is much less expensive than the NuPhy WH80, but its typing experience isn't as refined, and its software is a bit clunkier. It's also a viable step up from the MonsGeek FUN60 Ultra if you want better typing quality and a larger TKL layout with dedicated arrow keys and a function row.

Hey there SCheng,

I think it’s most helpful to present our Office score as a weighted assessment of specific aspects of performance associated with a good office keyboard, not necessarily a definitive ranking of the keyboards that we think will suit the greatest number of people for office use.

Our Office score is an interesting one as 65% of the score comes from Typing Quality (50%) and Ergonomics (15%). This can push some highly adjustable ergonomic boards and mechanical models with excellent typing experiences above some of the more conventional options currently included on this list. Our score tries to capture some of those advantages, although some of the keyboards that score above these more conventional models may involve a learning curve that not everyone is interested in confronting. One thing our experience has taught us is that most people visiting this article favor more familiar, conventional keyboards, often with low-profile scissor switches similar to those found on laptops. Ultimately, our office score also doesn’t factor in every aspect that influences our recommendations, including layout, appearance, and learning curve as well as other aspects like availability and price, so we rely on our own editorial judgement along those lines.

Referencing our table for an example, the GravaStar Mercury K1 receives an 8.5 Office score, higher than any of our current picks. However, its lack of a numpad, relatively loud typing noise, and unconventional appearance make it unlikely to be the best office keyboard for most people.

All that to say, our scores aim to provide an overview of specific aspects of performance, but they won’t automatically determine our recommendations.
