The Finalmouse Starlight X, also known as the Finalmouse SLX, is a lightweight wireless gaming mouse with a symmetrical shape. It features a similar shape to previous Finalmouse UltralightX and Starlight models, but with some minor tweaks. It has a mostly solid carbon-fiber composite shell instead of the honeycomb cutouts on the brand's UltralightX models. Its main buttons still use standard mechanical switches, but they add separate tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR) sensors to detect your inputs. This lets you choose how far you press before a click registers, and it turns on Rapid Trigger, which allows you to adjust the point at which the click resets. It connects through a USB receiver or a cable, and you set it up through Finalmouse's web-based software.

Our Verdict
N/AVideo Games (FPS) 

The Finalmouse SLX is an outstanding FPS gaming mouse. It's extremely lightweight, so you can flick and reposition it with very little effort. It also has incredibly low click latency and a remarkably accurate, responsive sensor, making it well-suited for competitive play at any level. Its main buttons let you adjust the actuation point and use Rapid Trigger, which gives you more control over your clicks and makes fast follow-up shots easier than on a conventional switch, especially with semi-automatic weapons. However, the most sensitive settings, where these features help most, can be hard to control, and the click you feel doesn't line up with where your input registers, which can feel disorienting.

Pros

Extremely lightweight.

Excellent build quality.

Incredibly low click latency.

Outstanding sensor performance.

Adjustable actuation point and Rapid Trigger.

Cons

Tactile click feedback is decoupled from the click actuation point in TMR analog mode, which could feel disorienting.

Popular Mouse Comparisons

The Finalmouse SLX is a lightweight wireless gaming mouse. It has largely the same shape as Finalmouse's recent releases from the UltralightX lineup with a few tweaks, and it uses the same carbon-fiber composite construction. The big differentiator is that it uses TMR sensors in its main buttons, which let you adjust where clicks register, and it also features Rapid Trigger, a setting that allows for faster repeated inputs. It also has what Finalmouse calls 'PerfectPolling,' which is a 1000Hz polling rate that delivers latency performance similar to 8000Hz polling rates.

The SLX competes with the high-end Logitech G PRO X3 SUPERSTRIKE and the Logitech G PRO X2 SUPERSTRIKE, which also have adjustable click actuation. The biggest difference is that the Logitech mice use haptic motors to simulate the feeling of mechanical clicks wherever you have the actuation point set to, whereas the Finalmouse still has mechanical switches, so the physical click sensation happens at the same point, no matter what you have the actuation point set to. This can feel unnatural compared to the Logitech's implementation.

Finalmouse also sells its mice in limited drops, so the SLX can be harder to buy than rivals that are readily available.

For more recommendations, see our picks for the best FPS mice, the best lightweight mice, and the best wireless gaming mice.

Logitech G PRO X3 SUPERSTRIKE
SEE PRICEAmazon

Both the Finalmouse Starlight X and the Logitech G PRO X3 SUPERSTRIKE are lightweight wireless gaming mice that let you adjust the actuation distance of the main buttons. Both perform exceptionally well and are well-suited for competitive play. The biggest difference is how they provide click feedback: the Finalmouse's mechanical tactile click occurs at a fixed point in the button travel regardless of your actuation setting, while the Logitech's adjustable haptic feedback simulates a click to match that setting, which can feel more natural. The Logitech also has better build quality with a fully enclosed plastic body. On the other hand, the Finalmouse is noticeably lighter, with a carbon-fiber composite body, small cutouts on top, and honeycomb cutouts underneath. It also has smoother-gliding feet.

The Finalmouse Starlight X and the Logitech G PRO X2 SUPERSTRIKE are lightweight gaming mice that both let you adjust where their clicks actuate and use Rapid Trigger, but they feel very different. The Finalmouse keeps a physical mechanical click, while the Logitech uses inductive switches with haptic motors that simulate one, with the advantage being that there's tactile feedback no matter where you set the actuation point. The Finalmouse is much lighter and better balanced, its PTFE feet glide more smoothly, and its click latency is lower. The Logitech has an even sturdier build, quieter clicks, and fully programmable buttons.

A bit late, but this is related to the click latency measurements:

In their video, Finalmouse argues that using a solenoid to actuate mouse buttons introduces unrealistic force and velocity, potentially skewing results in favour of mechanical/SPDT switches. Do you guys have any observations or response to this criticism?

From what I understand, end-to-end click latency can be broken down into:

Button pre-travel
Switch/internal actuation latency
PCB/firmware processing
Wired/wireless transmission The concern, is that if the solenoid moves too quickly or applies force differently from a human finger, it could compress the physical component of the measurement, potentially affecting different switch tech differently. However, I’m not entirely convinced that this is necessarily a significant issue with the setup.
From the videos I’ve seen, the solenoid doesn’t look dramatically faster than a human click, and it gets time to reset between clicks, unlike the actuator shown in the FM video. I also looked up the solenoid model and found that it’s rated for 6 N of force, which doesn’t seem particularly high compared to what a human finger can produce. In your earlier keyboard testing, you also mentioned that the solenoid was around 20% faster: I want to confirm of the solenoid force value and speed is what I listed above.
*** This brings me to the methodology itself. If the goal is to characterise the limits of the device itself, wouldn’t there be some value in testing beyond typical human input speeds? Rather than asking ‘how quickly can a person realistically click this mouse?’, the question would be ‘at what point does the device itself become the limiting factor?’ I’m not sure if that line of thinking is correct, but I’d like to understand how you guys approach this. Have you tested how changing the solenoid’s speed or force affects the results, particularly when comparing different switch technologies? There also another thought that I’ve heard from reviewers that 1. aka button pre-travel should be measured separately in length and not time. Any thoughts on this approach.

Hey there, great question! It’s pretty loaded too, I’ll do my best to answer all of it, but don’t hesitate to ask more questions! 

The short answer is: The speed at which the solenoid or the linear motor used to test the click latency will highly impact test results. And more so, digital mice will get a hefty penalty in their results when using slower actuators during test since they all have more pre-travel than analog mice, by design. However, while pre-travel does increase click latency (due to how we, and finalmouse, choose to include this in our test method), it is not a bad thing since it helps avoid having a mouse being too sensitive to misclicks which is a lot more penalizing in games when you fire without wanting too, than adding 1ms to your latency chain. The long answer explains more!

The long answer: 
The main difference between digital and analog mouse is what happens when the mouse triggers.

For digital, it’s a mechanical switch that needs to fully move for metal parts to come into contact and trigger the click. Since the click latency test includes pre-travel, the speed at which the actuator move will impact the test result.
For analog, the switch is always sending signals and will return a trigger signal when a certain distance threshold is reached. The distance for actuation is controlled by the software. In the Finalmouse video, the presenter sets this distance to their smallest possible pre-travel of 0.01mm(and there is a disclaimer now saying that anything below 0.1mm is intended for benchmarking only!). The Pro X2 Superstrike uses discrete distance instead (in levels, not millimeters, so it’s hard to compare). In the analog case, the speed of the actuator will not be nearly as important, since the mouse can be set up to have virtually no pre-travel.

The second thing to consider: Human speed is a range, not a defined value. Our solenoid moves fast, yes, and its at the upper range of the human speed range. Our solenoid is also not controlled in speed, but in force, so the actual speed at which the test is conducted is a complex calculations that is defined by a few test parameters. We have a methodology to reduce the variance of these factors, so all of our tests are conducted at similar speeds. For Finalmouse, their linear actuator is most likely controlled by speed. The presenter doesn’t say it outright, but it’s a common control scheme of linear actuator, so I assume it. Finalmouse also never mention their test speed. They just say they mimic better human speed, but are not basing those facts on available references either. That said, it’s very possible that we both test in the human speed range, while still having two different testing speed.

Considering that we can test at two different valid speeds, and considering how impactful the actuator speed is to account for pre-travel, it is completely normal for Finalmouse to prefer slower testing speed which advantages analog mouse for their video. And again, the difference in results between the Superlight X and the Pro X2 Superstrike might just well be their different pre-travel of 0.01mm VS whatever the Superlight level 1 distance is (likely 0.1mm).  One thing I don’t like from Finalmouse however is while they perform their test at 0.01mm actuation distance, they do not recommend playing at that settings since it makes the mouse too sensitive. This brings me to my next point. 

Logitech has published the presets of a few pro gamers using their Superstrike: https://www.logitechg.com/en-us/discover/learn/superstrike-hits-codes. As you can see, no pro player is playing at Level 1 actuation distance (the smallest). All pro players will prefer a mouse with slightly more pre-travel to avoid misclicks by sacrifice a little bit of click latency. The exception is of course rapid trigger, but this is not related to the click latency test. So the open question to ask here is if it is normal to test a mouse with incredibly small 0.01mm pre-travel for click latency if we know that is not how players use their mouse while gaming. 

This brings us to the RTINGS philosophy for test. We have a lot of driving test design principles, but there are two main ones at play here: 1) We want repeatable and comparable test results between products and 2) We want to test products the same way users use them. While there can be exception to these principles here and there, the click latency test follows both. In the case of the Finalmouse, we had a big internal discussion to see where we should set the actuation level, which we landed on 0.1mm in the end. This is the minimal value at which the mouse felt usable for gaming. Anything below that was way too prone to mistriggers and a real pain to use. This is the first difference between our test and the Finalmouse test. The second difference between our two test is the speed of the actuator, as already discussed. From my point of view, both test speeds are valid and properly mimic a human press. I see why Finalmouse would push for slower test speed, since it’s an advantage for them, but this is where being an independent tester shines, we can make our own decision :), and not be driven by another company’s agenda.

For anyone with doubt about our testing speed, we did a lengthy post comparing our solenoid test to human reaction test here 

This all said, as you can very well see the Starlight X click latency gets a perfect 10 and the 0.5ms measured click latency is one of the best we’ve ever seen. While having a higher speed lower click latency of other mice, it doesn’t penalize analog ones. 

Considering all that, while click latency is an important metric, it should not be the main focus when making a buying decision. As you can see from the Logitech Pro Players settings, pre-travel is a personal choice and it’s sometimes worth sacrificing click latency over it. Games are not decided down to 1-2ms of click latency, as marketing often presents it. 

The last question about Pre-Travel is an interesting one. We’ve thought many times to add it to our review, but never implemented it since users don’t seem to care or talk about it. It used to be a set value based on the mouse design, but with analog mouse and their variable actuation point, it might be something that more users think about, and now know what to do with the information. My take on it is that pre-travel is a big feeling thing: Some users will like their mouse very sensitive with close to no pre-travel, while others will prefer deep mouse strokes. Just like keyboards. Previously, we could have shown that value, but we could never score it. Users would have been left with a value, and not much way to understand how that value would affect them. Any people fortunate enough to have been able to use the adjustable Superstrike or Starlight, or have tried many different mice, might understand what they prefer as pre-travel value. And to directly answer your question, it would definitely be a distance not a speed. This should now be clearer with what I exposed about the test method above. 

Hopefully this was a good read! Let me know if you have any more questions!
